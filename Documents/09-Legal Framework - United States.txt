READ THIS FIRST
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
This document is a research index, not legal advice and not a court order. It lists
United States federal and state authorities that counsel may evaluate if the facts
alleged in Documents 01-08 are proven. Listing a statute does not mean it applies,
that a claim will succeed, or that any court has ruled. Several authorities apply
only if a person is legally an employee rather than an independent contractor, and
several require administrative exhaustion or contractual arbitration. Confirm the
current text and limitations period with a licensed attorney in the relevant state.

The business is assumed to operate in the United States for this document. State
law examples are illustrative, not exhaustive.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. THRESHOLD: EMPLOYEE OR INDEPENDENT CONTRACTOR
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Most employment statutes protect "employees", not independent contractors. The
first question in any claim is classification.

- FLSA "economic realities" test: whether the worker is economically dependent on
  the business. See Goldberg v. Whitaker House Cooperative, 366 U.S. 28 (1961);
  United States v. Silk, 331 U.S. 704 (1947).
- IRS common-law control test: Rev. Rul. 87-41 (20 factors); IRS Publication 15-A.
- California ABC test: Dynamex Operations West, Inc. v. Superior Court, 4 Cal. 5th
  903 (2018), codified at Cal. Lab. Code § 2775.
- New York: scattered-factor common-law test; NY State Freelance Isn't Free Act,
  Labor Law § 181 (effective 2024) gives freelancers written-contract, timely-payment
  and anti-retaliation rights regardless of employee status.
- California Freelance Worker Protection Act (2024, effective 2025) adds written
  contract and payment protections for freelance workers.
- New Jersey ABC test: N.J.S.A. 43:21-19(i)(6).

Why it matters here: if contributors are misclassified, wage, overtime, retaliation,
discrimination and workers' compensation exposure can follow, plus state penalties.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2. WAGE AND HOUR
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
- Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA), 29 U.S.C. §§ 201-219.
  - Minimum wage, § 206.
  - Overtime, § 207.
  - Anti-retaliation, § 215(a)(3).
  - Willful violation, 3-year limitations period, § 255(a).
  - Collective action, § 216(b).
- State analogues (examples):
  - California: Lab. Code §§ 510 (overtime), 1194 (civil action), 226 (itemized
    statements), 2802 (expenses).
  - New York: Labor Law §§ 650 et seq. (minimum wage), 190 et seq. (frequency of
    payment).
  - Washington: RCW 49.46 (Minimum Wage Act); RCW 49.52 (willful withholding).
  - Texas: Tex. Lab. Code ch. 61 (Payday Law).
- Unpaid wages, withheld final pay, chargebacks and "volunteer" work can all be
  framed as wage claims if an employment relationship exists.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3. RETALIATION AND WHISTLEBLOWING
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
- FLSA retaliation, 29 U.S.C. § 215(a)(3).
- Title VII retaliation, 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-3(a).
- ADA retaliation, 42 U.S.C. § 12203.
- ADEA retaliation, 29 U.S.C. § 623(d).
- FMLA retaliation, 29 U.S.C. § 2615.
- NLRA protected concerted activity, 29 U.S.C. §§ 157, 158(a)(1) (employees only;
  independent contractors are excluded).
- False Claims Act retaliation, 31 U.S.C. § 3730(h).
- Sarbanes-Oxley whistleblower, 18 U.S.C. § 1514A (public companies).
- Dodd-Frank whistleblower, 15 U.S.C. § 78u-6.
- State whistleblower statutes (examples):
  - California: Lab. Code § 1102.5.
  - New York: Lab. Law § 740 (and § 741 for health care).
  - Texas: Tex. Lab. Code § 451.
  - Illinois: Whistleblower Act, 740 ILCS 174.
  - Washington: RCW 49.60.210; RCW 42.40 (state employees).

Mapping: banning a member after they raise a contract or safety concern is the
core retaliation theory. Success depends on protected activity, causation and
timing, and on employee status where the statute requires it.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4. DISCRIMINATION, HARASSMENT AND LEAVE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
- Title VII, 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-2 (race, color, religion, sex, national origin).
- ADA, 42 U.S.C. § 12112 (disability); § 12203 (retaliation).
- ADEA, 29 U.S.C. § 623 (age 40+).
- Equal Pay Act, 29 U.S.C. § 206(d).
- GINA, 42 U.S.C. § 2000ff et seq. (genetic information).
- FMLA, 29 U.S.C. § 2601 et seq. (50-employee threshold; leave and retaliation).
- State civil-rights statutes:
  - California FEHA, Gov. Code § 12900 et seq.
  - New York Human Rights Law, Exec. Law § 296.
  - Washington Law Against Discrimination, RCW 49.60.
  - Texas Commission on Human Rights Act, Tex. Lab. Code ch. 21.
- Hostile-environment and harassment theories require severe or pervasive conduct
  and, in some cases, employer knowledge and failure to act.

The archive describes a hostile environment created by meetings and penalties. If
the affected people are employees, these statutes are the usual route. If they are
contractors, contract, tort and consumer theories are more likely.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5. CONTRACT, RESTITUTION AND GOOD FAITH
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
- Breach of contract: common law; Restatement (Second) of Contracts §§ 1, 235, 347.
- Promissory estoppel: Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 90.
- Implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing: Restatement § 205; state law
  (e.g., California, New York).
- Unjust enrichment / quantum meruit: Restatement (Third) of Restitution and Unjust
  Enrichment § 1; state common law.
- Statute of Frauds: Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 110; state statutes.
- UCC Article 2 applies to goods, not services; game development and freelancing
  are usually common-law services.
- Procuring cause / commission disputes: state broker and sales-representative
  statutes (e.g., California, New York) may apply to unpaid commissions.

Mapping: unpaid invoices, revoked credit, withheld deliverables and "we will pay
when we can" assurances fit here. Written contracts, invoices, statements of work,
Discord agreements and payment records are the evidence that matters.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
6. FRAUD, MISREPRESENTATION AND CONSUMER PROTECTION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
- Common-law fraudulent misrepresentation: Restatement (Second) of Torts § 525
  (knowing or reckless false statement, intent to induce reliance, justifiable
  reliance, damages).
- Negligent misrepresentation: Restatement (Second) of Torts § 552.
- Concealment: Restatement (Second) of Torts § 550.
- State consumer-protection statutes (examples):
  - California: Unfair Competition Law, Bus. & Prof. Code § 17200; Consumers Legal
    Remedies Act, Civ. Code § 1750 et seq.; false advertising, Bus. & Prof. Code
    § 17500.
  - New York: Gen. Bus. Law § 349 (deceptive acts), § 350 (false advertising).
  - Texas: Deceptive Trade Practices Act, Tex. Bus. & Com. Code § 17.46.
  - Washington: Consumer Protection Act, RCW 19.86.
  - Illinois: Consumer Fraud and Deceptive Business Practices Act, 815 ILCS 505.
- Federal: FTC Act § 5, 15 U.S.C. § 45 (unfair or deceptive acts or practices).
- Securities fraud (if investment-like): 15 U.S.C. § 78j(b); SEC Rule 10b-5,
  17 C.F.R. § 240.10b-5.
- Criminal fraud: 18 U.S.C. § 1341 (mail), § 1343 (wire), § 1349 (conspiracy).

Mapping: claims that work was original when it was not, that funds were for
development when they were diverted, or that a contributor would be credited or
paid, can support fraud theories if knowledge and reliance are proven. "Fraud"
must be pleaded with particularity under Fed. R. Civ. P. 9(b).

7. INTELLECTUAL PROPERTY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Copyright
- Copyright Act, 17 U.S.C. § 101 et seq.
- Ownership and works made for hire: § 201, § 101; Community for Creative
  Non-Violence v. Reid, 490 U.S. 730 (1989).
- Joint authorship: § 101; Aalmuhammed v. Lee, 202 F.3d 1227 (9th Cir. 2000).
- Derivative works: § 101.
- Registration is a prerequisite to suit for US works: § 411(a); Fourth Estate
  Public Benefit Corp. v. Wall-Street.com, 586 U.S. 296 (2019).
- Statutory damages and attorney's fees: § 504, § 412 (timing of registration).
- Three-year limitations period: § 507(b).
- DMCA anti-circumvention: § 1201; copyright management information: § 1202;
  safe harbor: § 512.
- Digital performance: § 106(6); compulsory license issues for game soundtracks.

Trade secrets
- Defend Trade Secrets Act, 18 U.S.C. § 1836 et seq.; definition § 1839(3);
  three-year limitation § 1836(d).
- Uniform Trade Secrets Act, adopted in most states.

Trademark and unfair competition
- Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1051 et seq.; § 1114 (registered marks); § 1125(a)
  (false designation of origin, false advertising).
- State trademark and unfair-competition statutes.

Right of publicity
- State law, e.g., Cal. Civ. Code § 3344; N.Y. Civ. Rights Law §§ 50-51.
- Relevant if contributors' names, likenesses or credits are used or erased.

Mapping: the ChilloutVR framework, ported worlds, "Claude rewrite", stripped
credits and allegedly appropriated code are the core IP theories. Ownership turns
on contracts, licenses and work-for-hire terms, which the archive says are absent
or disputed.

8. COMPUTER ACCESS, PRIVACY AND DATA
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
- Computer Fraud and Abuse Act, 18 U.S.C. § 1030; civil action § 1030(g);
  "exceeds authorized access" narrowed in Van Buren v. United States, 593 U.S. 374
  (2021).
- Electronic Communications Privacy Act, 18 U.S.C. §§ 2510-2523; interception
  § 2511. One-party vs two-party consent varies by state.
- Stored Communications Act, 18 U.S.C. §§ 2701-2713.
- State computer-crime statutes, e.g., Cal. Penal Code § 502.
- Children's Online Privacy Protection Act, 15 U.S.C. §§ 6501-6506; FTC Rule
  16 C.F.R. Part 312.
- State privacy statutes:
  - California Consumer Privacy Act / CPRA, Cal. Civ. Code § 1798.100 et seq.
  - Washington My Health My Data Act, RCW 19.373.
  - Illinois Biometric Information Privacy Act, 740 ILCS 14.
- Fourth Amendment and state constitutional privacy: Katz v. United States, 389
  U.S. 347 (1967); Carpenter v. United States, 585 U.S. 296 (2018).

Mapping: the allegation that a server runs hacking tools with full access to
connected PCs is the most serious technical claim. It requires forensic evidence
(logs, configurations, access records) and should be referred to counsel and law
enforcement before publication of technical details.

9. ECONOMIC TORTS, RICO AND ANTITRUST
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
- Tortious interference with contract/prospective advantage: Restatement (Second)
  of Torts §§ 766, 766B.
- Trade libel / commercial disparagement: Restatement § 623A, § 626.
- Unfair competition: state common law and statutes.
- RICO: 18 U.S.C. §§ 1961-1968; § 1962(c) (pattern of racketeering), § 1962(d)
  (conspiracy), § 1964(c) (civil treble damages); predicate acts include wire fraud
  § 1343, mail fraud § 1341. Four-year limitation: Agency Holding Corp. v.
  Malley-Duff & Associates, 483 U.S. 143 (1987).
- Sherman Act, 15 U.S.C. §§ 1-2; group boycott and concerted refusal to deal;
  Clayton Act § 4, 15 U.S.C. § 15 (treble damages); § 4B, 15 U.S.C. § 15b
  (four-year limitation).
- State antitrust and unfair-competition statutes.

Mapping: RICO and antitrust are high-threshold theories. They are included because
the archive alleges coordinated exclusion of multiple organisations and misuse of
funds, not because they are easy to prove.

10. DEFAMATION AND PUBLICATION RISK
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
This repository publishes allegations. That creates its own legal exposure, which
must be managed alongside the claims against Parelllc.

- Defamation elements: Restatement (Second) of Torts § 558 (false and defamatory
  statement of fact, published, fault, damages).
- Public figures and actual malice: New York Times Co. v. Sullivan, 376 U.S. 254
  (1964); Gertz v. Robert Welch, Inc., 418 U.S. 323 (1974).
- Opinion vs fact: Milkovich v. Lorain Journal Co., 497 U.S. 1 (1990).
- Fair report privilege: Restatement § 611 (reports of official proceedings).
- Neutral reportage is not accepted in every circuit.
- Section 230, 47 U.S.C. § 230, protects platforms for user content but does NOT
  protect the person who posts their own defamatory statement.
- Anti-SLAPP protections (examples): Cal. Code Civ. Proc. § 425.16; Texas Citizens
  Participation Act, Tex. Civ. Prac. & Rem. Code § 27.001 et seq.; N.Y. Civ. Rights
  Law §§ 70-a, 76-a (2020 amendments); Uniform Public Expression Protection Act
  adopted in some states (e.g., Wash. RCW 4.105).

Safe practice for this repository:
- State facts as sourced allegations, with dates and document references.
- Avoid asserting criminal guilt as fact; use "alleged".
- Separate opinion from fact ("In my opinion...").
- Publish only legal names that are public or consented to; otherwise use the
  username.
- Keep the right-of-reply notice prominent and act on corrections quickly.

11. PROCEDURE, LIMITATIONS AND ARBITRATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
- Federal question jurisdiction: 28 U.S.C. § 1331.
- Diversity jurisdiction: 28 U.S.C. § 1332 (complete diversity; $75,000 amount in
  controversy).
- Supplemental jurisdiction: 28 U.S.C. § 1367.
- Venue: 28 U.S.C. § 1391; transfer § 1404.
- Personal jurisdiction: International Shoe Co. v. Washington, 326 U.S. 310 (1945);
  Bristol-Myers Squibb Co. v. Superior Court, 582 U.S. 255 (2017); Ford Motor Co.
  v. Montana Eighth Judicial District, 592 U.S. 351 (2021).
- Class actions: Fed. R. Civ. P. 23; FLSA collective actions § 216(b).
- Arbitration: Federal Arbitration Act, 9 U.S.C. §§ 1-16; Epic Systems Corp. v.
  Lewis, 584 U.S. 497 (2018). Check every contract for an arbitration clause and
  class-action waiver.
- Administrative exhaustion: EEOC charge before Title VII/ADA/ADEA suit, 42 U.S.C.
  § 2000e-5; state agencies may have worksharing agreements.
- Limitations periods (examples; verify):
  - FLSA: 2 years, 3 if willful, 29 U.S.C. § 255(a).
  - Title VII/ADA: 180 or 300 days to file with EEOC, then 90 days to sue,
    42 U.S.C. § 2000e-5(e)(1), (f)(1).
  - ADEA: 180/300 days, 29 U.S.C. § 626(d).
  - Copyright: 3 years, 17 U.S.C. § 507(b).
  - DTSA: 3 years, 18 U.S.C. § 1836(d).
  - RICO: 4 years.
  - Antitrust: 4 years, 15 U.S.C. § 15b.
  - § 1983: borrows state personal-injury period (often 2-3 years).
  - Fraud and contract: state-specific, commonly 2-6 years.

12. GAME-SPECIFIC AND PLATFORM ISSUES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
- Steam Distribution Agreement: a contract between the publisher and Valve; breach,
  termination and content warranties are contractual, not statutory.
- ESRB ratings and platform content rules: ESRB is industry self-regulation; platform
  agreements can require compliance.
- COPPA (children's data) and state age-verification laws.
- Consumer refund and false-advertising exposure: FTC Act § 5; state UDAP statutes;
  EU/UK consumer rules if sold there (see Document 10).
- Loot boxes and gambling: state gambling statutes and FTC scrutiny; platform
  disclosure rules.
- Online safety: UK Online Safety Act 2023 and EU Digital Services Act (Document 10)
  apply to services offered in those markets, regardless of US incorporation.
- Asset licensing: Unreal, Unity, Fab, Sketchfab and similar licences restrict
  redistribution; violations are copyright and contract claims.

14. EVIDENCE CHECKLIST FOR COUNSEL
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
- Signed contracts, statements of work, NDAs, licences and EULAs.
- Invoices, payment records, bank/PayPal/Stripe statements and refunds.
- Discord exports with timestamps, user IDs and server IDs; preserve metadata.
- Emails, DMs and voicemails; do not edit originals.
- Git commit history, file metadata and asset provenance.
- Server logs, access logs and configuration evidence (forensic image if possible).
- Screenshots with capture time and URL/context.
- Witness statements from other organisations and members.
- Evidence of credit removal (before/after pages, archives, Wayback captures).
- Steam/Valve correspondence and publication records.
- Proof of registration for copyrights and trademarks.
- Medical, financial or reputational harm records, if claimed.
