READ THIS FIRST
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
This is a research index, not legal advice and not a court order. It lists statutes,
regulations and instruments that counsel may evaluate if the facts alleged in
Documents 01-12 are proven. Listing an instrument does not mean it applies, that a
claim will succeed, or that any court or regulator has ruled. Applicability depends
on where the affected person works, where the service is offered, the contracts in
place and the choice-of-law clauses. Verify the current text with a lawyer qualified
in the relevant jurisdiction.

Common-law jurisdictions covered: United Kingdom, Canada (common-law provinces),
Australia, New Zealand and Ireland. The European Union is included because it sets
mandatory rules for services offered to EU users, and because Ireland is both
common-law and EU. Quebec and Scotland are mixed/civil-law systems and are noted
where relevant.

Section 8A applies the working-time and notice authorities to the 3 November 2026
same-day mandatory meeting and strike sequence in Document 12.

1. UNITED KINGDOM
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Employment and worker status
- Employment Rights Act 1996 (ERA 1996):
  - Unfair dismissal, s.94; qualifying period generally 2 years, s.108.
  - Automatically unfair dismissal for whistleblowing, s.103A.
  - Detriment for whistleblowing, s.47B; remedy s.48.
  - Written particulars, s.1.
- Public Interest Disclosure Act 1998 (inserted the whistleblowing provisions into
  ERA 1996).
- Working Time Regulations 1998 (SI 1998/1833).
- National Minimum Wage Act 1998.
- Employment Relations Act 1999.
- Trade Union and Labour Relations (Consolidation) Act 1992.
- Status: "worker" is broader than "employee" (Uber BV v Aslam [2021] UKSC 5).
  Many gig and freelance arrangements can still attract worker protections.

Equality and harassment
- Equality Act 2010 (protected characteristics; harassment ss.26-27; victimisation
  s.27).
- Protection from Harassment Act 1997 (civil and criminal; course of conduct).

Commercial and corporate
- Companies Act 2006: directors' duties ss.171-177, including s.172 (promote the
  success of the company) and s.174 (reasonable care, skill and diligence).
- Insolvency Act 1986: s.212 (misfeasance), s.214 (wrongful trading), s.238-239
  (transactions at an undervalue/preferences).
- Bribery Act 2010.
- Modern Slavery Act 2015 (supply-chain statements).
- Fraud Act 2006: s.1 fraud; s.2 false representation; s.3 failing to disclose;
  s.4 abuse of position.
- Computer Misuse Act 1990: ss.1-3 (unauthorised access, unauthorised acts,
  unauthorised access with intent).
- Consumer Rights Act 2015 (digital content; goods; services).
- Online Safety Act 2023 (illegal and harmful content; age assurance).

Intellectual property and data
- Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988 (CDPA 1988).
- Trade Marks Act 1994.
- Defamation Act 2013 (serious harm threshold, s.1; truth s.2; honest opinion s.3;
  public interest s.4; website operators s.5).
- Data Protection Act 2018 and UK GDPR.
- Limitation Act 1980: contract and tort generally 6 years; defamation 1 year.

  reduction and refund.
- EU Digital Content Directive (EU) 2019/770: conformity, remedies, modification
  rights.
- EU/UK consumer protection: misleading advertising, dark patterns and unfair terms.

8A. SAME-DAY MEETINGS, REST AND UNILATERAL PENALTIES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
This section applies the framework to the 3 November 2026 sequence in Document 12.
It does not assert that any claim succeeds. It identifies the issues counsel should
evaluate in the complainant's and the company's jurisdictions.

(a) The "emergency meeting" label does not decide the legal question
- The legal question is not what the meeting was called. It is whether the meeting
  was scheduled, whether the notice was reasonable, whether attendance was
  compulsory, whether the person was on leave, and what consequence attached to
  non-attendance.
- The 3 November record shows a same-day announcement, a same-day ping, a
  pre-existing leave notice, a written strike threat and a strike notice. Those are
  the facts a tribunal would examine.

(b) Working time and rest
- UK: Working Time Regulations 1998 (SI 1998/1833): 48-hour average week (reg. 4),
  11-hour daily rest (reg. 10), 24-hour weekly rest (reg. 11), rest breaks (reg. 12),
  night-work limits (regs. 6-7). A mandatory meeting that cuts into rest can engage
  these rules for workers. The "worker" category is broader than "employee" (Uber
  BV v Aslam [2021] UKSC 5).
- Canada: Canada Labour Code Part III (hours of work, rest periods, overtime) for
  federally regulated employers; Ontario ESA 2000 (hours of work, rest periods,
  overtime, reprisal); Quebec Act respecting labour standards. Common-law
  reasonable-notice and good-faith duties may also apply.
- Australia: Fair Work Act 2009 s.62 (maximum weekly hours), s.65 (flexible working
  requests), Part 3-1 (general protections); state and territory WHS laws on
  psychosocial hazards.
- New Zealand: Employment Relations Act 2000 (good faith, unjustified
  disadvantage); Holidays Act 2003; Health and Safety at Work Act 2015
  (psychosocial hazards).
- Ireland: Organisation of Working Time Act 1997 (rest periods, maximum hours);
  Unfair Dismissals Act 1977; Protected Disclosures Act 2014.
- EU: Working Time Directive 2003/88/EC (11-hour daily rest, 24-hour weekly rest,
  rest breaks, 48-hour week); Directive (EU) 2019/1152 on transparent and
  predictable working conditions; Charter Art. 31; Platform Work Directive
  (EU) 2024/2831.

(c) Notice, predictability and unilateral variation
- EU Directive 2019/1152 requires transparent and predictable working conditions,
  including advance notice of work schedules for unpredictable work. A same-day
  mandatory meeting is the opposite of predictable scheduling.
- UK: ERA 1996 s.1 (written particulars); ACAS Code of Practice on disciplinary and
  grievance procedures (fairness, notice, opportunity to respond, right to be
  accompanied). A disciplinary penalty imposed without notice or a chance to explain
  is a procedural-fairness problem even where the statutory threshold is not met.
- Canada: ESA 2000 (written notice of hours and reprisal); common-law duty of good
  faith and fair dealing.
- Australia: Fair Work Act 2009 s.62 and Part 3-1; unfair contract terms in the ACL
  for standard-form contracts.
- Contract: unilateral variation, breach of the implied covenant of good faith,
  promissory estoppel.

(d) Unlawful deductions and adverse action
- UK: ERA 1996 s.13 (unlawful deduction from wages); s.47B (whistleblowing
  detriment); WTR 1998 enforcement.
- Canada: ESA 2000 (wage payment, reprisal); Canada Labour Code Part III.
- Australia: Fair Work Act 2009 Part 3-1 (adverse action), s.323 (method and
  frequency of payment), s.325 (unreasonable deductions).
- New Zealand: Employment Relations Act 2000 (unjustified disadvantage); Wages
  Protection Act 1983.
- Ireland: Payment of Wages Act 1991 (unlawful deductions); Organisation of Working
  Time Act 1997.
- EU: Directive 2019/1152; national wage-payment and deduction laws.
- A "strike" that withholds pay, points, access or continued engagement may be an
  unlawful deduction or adverse action if the person is a worker or employee. If
  the person is a contractor, contract and restitution theories take over.

(e) Retaliation and protected disclosure
- UK: ERA 1996 ss.47B, 103A; Public Interest Disclosure Act 1998.
- Canada: ESA 2000 reprisal; Canada Labour Code; provincial whistleblower statutes.
- Australia: Fair Work Act 2009 Part 3-1; state whistleblower protections.
- New Zealand: Protected Disclosures Act 2000; ERA 2000 personal grievance.
- Ireland: Protected Disclosures Act 2014.
- EU: Directive (EU) 2019/1937 on whistleblowers (where implemented).
- The protected activity is the complainant's written objection to the policy and
  the request for a written contract (Document 12, Exhibits P-04 and P-05). The
  adverse action is the strike record and the asset-removal threat (P-06 and P-07).

(f) Misrepresentation and defamation
- UK: Fraud Act 2006 ss.2-4; Misrepresentation Act 1967; Defamation Act 2013.
- Canada: Criminal Code s.380; provincial libel and slander Acts; Competition Act
  (false advertising).
- Australia: ACL ss.18, 29; state defamation Acts.
- New Zealand: Fair Trading Act 1986 ss.9, 13; Defamation Act 1992.
- Ireland: Defamation Act 2009; Consumer Protection Act 2007.
- EU: Unfair Commercial Practices Directive 2005/29/EC; national defamation law.
- The statement "there was no strikes given" is contradicted by the written
  announcement and the strike notice. If relied on, it may support a
  misrepresentation or misleading-conduct theory.

(g) Property and asset removal
- Conversion, detinue and trespass to chattels are common-law torts in the UK,
  Canada, Australia, New Zealand and Ireland. The exact name and elements vary.
- A threat to remove a contractor's stored assets is not itself a taking. A claim
  requires proof that assets were removed, withheld or destroyed, or that the
  threat caused measurable harm.
- If assets were actually taken, criminal theft or fraud provisions may also be
  relevant (for example UK Fraud Act 2006; Canada Criminal Code s.322 theft and
  s.380 fraud; Australia Criminal Code Div 134; NZ Crimes Act 1961 ss.219, 240;
  Ireland Criminal Justice (Theft and Fraud Offences) Act 2001).
- Preserve the asset list, storage records, deletion notices, access logs and any
  written demand for return.

10. HUMAN-RIGHTS AND BUSINESS-CONDUCT FRAMING
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Where a statute does not fit, the archive may still rely on human-rights and
business-conduct standards in advocacy, regulatory complaints and ESG reporting:

- Right to fair working conditions: UDHR Art. 23; ICESCR Art. 7; EU Charter
  Art. 31.
- Freedom of expression and association: UDHR Arts. 19-20; ICCPR Arts. 19, 22;
  ECHR Arts. 10-11.
- Privacy and data protection: UDHR Art. 12; ICCPR Art. 17; GDPR; ECHR Art. 8.
- Property and credit: UDHR Art. 17; national copyright and contract law.
- Business and human rights: UN Guiding Principles (2011); OECD Guidelines (2023).
- Labour rights: ILO core conventions.
- Rest and reasonable working time: UDHR Art. 24; ICESCR Art. 7; European Social
  Charter Art. 2.

These instruments are persuasive and, in some countries, directly enforceable
against public bodies. Against a private company they are strongest when combined
with a domestic statute, a regulator complaint or a platform policy report.
